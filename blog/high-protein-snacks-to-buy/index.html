High-Protein Snacks to Buy: Compare 6 Everyday Formats

The useful high-protein snack is one that provides a meaningful amount of protein in the portion you actually eat and fits the rest of your diet. Before choosing a protein bar, yogurt, roasted bean snack or gummy, check the amount and protein source in the full serving. There is no single best snack for every shopper.

By Velq Editorial · General nutrition and label education

Six protein snack formats to look for in stores

Compare formats, not unverified brand rankings

Greek yogurt or cottage cheeseCompare protein, added sugars, dairy allergens and portion size. Keep refrigerated as directed.
Roasted edamame or chickpeasCheck protein, dietary fiber and sodium. Choose packages suitable for your storage conditions.
Protein barsCompare protein source, full bar size, added sugars and fiber rather than only front-of-pack claims.

Jerky or meat snacksCheck protein and sodium per serving; follow opening and storage directions.

Compare the true cost per gram of protein

Use the number of complete servings you would eat. Suppose a hypothetical six-bar box costs $12 and each bar has 15 g protein. Its cost per serving is $2 and its cost per gram is about 13 cents. A hypothetical four-pouch snack costing $12 with 20 g protein per pouch costs $3 per serving, or 15 cents per gram. These examples illustrate the method and are not current brand prices.

The calculation

Cost per serving = package price ÷ number of servings. Cost per gram = cost per serving ÷ protein grams per serving. Neither number alone establishes protein quality, suitability or overall value.

For your own products, try the free label-value calculator. Review the FDA's serving size explanation before comparing.

What about protein quality and fiber?

Check the named protein ingredient and consider the variety of protein foods in your diet. A number on the front of a package is not a complete picture of the amino acids, other nutrients or suitability of a food. Dietary fiber is a separate nutrient, so do not assume every protein snack is a source of fiber.

Look for suitable fiber-containing foods to pair with your snack, such as berries, oats or whole-grain crackers. The protein and fiber snack guide offers ideas for work, travel and plant-based routines. The FDA's nutrition label guide explains how to compare nutrients in packaged foods.

Choose based on where you will eat

For a home or office with a refrigerator, chilled foods can be practical. If you are traveling or carrying a snack all day, choose a product explicitly suitable for room-temperature storage and follow instructions after opening. The USDA's perishable food safety guidance is useful when refrigeration is uncertain. See our shelf-stable protein and fiber snack examples for more combinations.

Frequently asked questions

How much protein should a snack contain?

There is no universal snack target. Compare the amount in the serving you intend to eat with your overall dietary needs.

Are protein gummies better than protein bars?

Not inherently. Ingredients, protein quality, serving size, storage and other nutrients vary by product.

Should every protein snack also contain fiber?

No. You can get fiber from other foods throughout the day. Compare both nutrient amounts and choose a varied diet.
